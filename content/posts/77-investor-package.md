A letter. A one-pager. A deck link. Three documents in an email that Rick Moore will open sometime this week. Everything he needs to decide whether Humanity and AI is a real company doing a real thing, or just another AI pitch in a stack of AI pitches.

The package is ready. The question underneath the package is the question that every builder eventually faces: at what point do you stop building and start selling?

## The three documents

The letter is personal. It says who David is, what he's building, why it matters, and what he's asking for. The letter doesn't pitch the business — it introduces the person. Rick Moore invests in people before he invests in businesses, and the letter is written for that preference. Three paragraphs. No jargon. No hyperbole. Here's who I am. Here's what I've done. Here's what I'd like to talk about.

The one-pager is the business compressed to a single page. The thesis: specific AI tools for specific human activities. The products: five deployed, serving users, generating revenue. The technology: sixteen shared Foundation components enabling rapid deployment. The numbers: $7.99 from quilters, $4.99 from readers, two revenue streams with zero market overlap. The ask: a specific amount for a specific purpose — twelve months of runway to grow the existing products and deploy three more.

The deck link goes to a slide presentation that expands on the one-pager. Market size for each product category. Competitive landscape. Technology architecture. Financial projections. Team — which, at this point, is David and the AI collaboration that built everything. The deck is what Rick clicks through if the letter makes him curious and the one-pager makes him think.

Three documents. Escalating detail. Letter to one-pager to deck. Each document filters for a different level of interest. The letter is for everyone. The one-pager is for the interested. The deck is for the serious.

## The building-selling boundary

There's a moment in every venture when the builder has to become a seller. Not because building is done — building is never done. But because building without capital eventually reaches a boundary: the products work, the revenue exists, but the scale requires money that operations don't yet generate.

David has been building for months. Sixty-nine posts before this week. Five deployed products. Sixteen Foundation components. Revenue from two products. A social media pipeline. A policy brief. All of it built in a sprint-based collaboration that has produced more output in two months than most early-stage startups produce in a year.

The building has been the focus because building is what David does and what the collaboration excels at. The specify-build-review-iterate loop is productive and engaging. Each sprint produces something tangible. The process has momentum.

Selling has different dynamics. Selling is not a loop — it's a funnel. You contact many people. Some respond. Fewer meet. Fewer still invest. The ratio of effort to outcome is low compared to building, where every sprint produces an artifact. In selling, most conversations produce nothing except the information that this particular person isn't the right fit.

The investor package is the moment where building meets selling. The letter is written. The one-pager is formatted. The deck is linked. The package goes to Rick Moore, and then it goes to others. The building doesn't stop — there are sprints to run, products to improve, content to create. But a portion of attention now goes to the funnel.

## What the package represents

The package represents translation. The same translation problem that TasteBud's shopping list solves — converting aspiration to actionable reality — but in the domain of fundraising.

The aspiration: Humanity and AI is a viable company with a defensible thesis, working products, and a path to significant revenue. The reality that an investor needs: specific numbers, a clear business model, evidence of traction, a credible team, and a defined use of funds.

The letter translates David into a credible founder. The one-pager translates the project into a business. The deck translates the business into an investment opportunity. Each document is a layer of translation between what Humanity and AI is — a complex, multi-dimensional, AI-human collaborative operation — and what an investor needs to see: a bet worth making.

Translation always loses something. The one-pager can't capture the experience of watching five products deploy in a single day. The deck can't convey what it feels like to discover that specifications are the bottleneck, not tokens. The letter can't transmit the seventy-five posts worth of learning that preceded it. The package is a compression of a rich, complex experience into the format that capital requires.

This compression is necessary. Rick Moore doesn't have time to read seventy-five posts. He has time for a letter, a one-pager, and maybe a deck. If those three documents do their job, he'll have a meeting. If the meeting goes well, he'll do diligence. If diligence confirms what the documents claim, he'll invest. The funnel is long and narrow. The package is the opening of the funnel.

## The evidence problem

The unusual thing about Humanity and AI's investor package is the evidence available to support it.

Most early-stage pitches rely on projections — we believe this market is this big, we believe we can capture this percentage, we believe the revenue will grow at this rate. The projections are necessary because the company hasn't done enough to generate actual evidence. The pitch is about potential, not performance.

Humanity and AI has evidence. Five deployed products. Actual users. Actual revenue — small, but real. Seventy-five blog posts documenting the development process. Sixteen Foundation components that can be examined. A sprint methodology that's been iterated over hundreds of sprints. This isn't a pitch about what might happen. It's a pitch about what has happened, with a request for resources to make more of it happen.

The evidence changes the conversation. Instead of "we think we can build this," the pitch is "we've built this, and here it is." Instead of "we believe this model works," the pitch is "here are seventy-five posts documenting the model working in real time." Instead of "we project this revenue," the pitch is "here's the revenue, and here's why we think it grows with capital."

Evidence doesn't guarantee investment. The market might be too small. The team might be too thin. The products might not scale. Rick Moore might have a thesis that doesn't include civic technology or AI-human collaboration. Evidence reduces uncertainty but doesn't eliminate it. The package is an argument, not a proof.

## The question underneath

At what point do you stop building and start selling?

The honest answer is: you don't stop building. You can't. The products need improvement. The content needs creation. The sprints need running. Building is the activity that produces the thing worth selling. You don't stop building any more than a restaurant stops cooking when it starts accepting reservations.

But you do start selling. And starting to sell means accepting that some of the attention that was going to building will now go to pitching, to meetings, to follow-ups, to the slow and uncertain process of convincing someone with capital that your thing is worth their money.

The investor package is the beginning of this shift. Not a pivot — building continues. Not a distraction — selling is necessary. A shift in how attention is allocated. Some percentage of the next month's sprints will produce pitch materials instead of product features. Some percentage of David's time will go to meetings instead of specifications. Some percentage of the collaboration's output will serve the funnel instead of the product.

## The email

The package is in an email. The email goes to Rick Moore. Rick Moore opens it or doesn't. Reads the letter or doesn't. Clicks the one-pager or doesn't. Clicks the deck or doesn't. Responds or doesn't.

This is the uncertainty of selling. Building has a feedback loop — you deploy and it either works or it doesn't. Selling has a silence — you send and you wait. The package goes into the funnel and the funnel is opaque. You can't see where the email is. You can't see whether Rick is reading. You can't iterate on the pitch in real time the way you iterate on a sprint.

The package is ready. The letter is personal and honest. The one-pager is clear and specific. The deck is comprehensive and credible. The evidence is real — five products, actual revenue, seventy-five posts of documentation. The question is answered: you start selling when the package is ready and the building has produced enough to sell.

The email is sent. The building continues. The funnel opens. Everything that happens next depends on whether three documents, compressed from months of work, convince one person to have one meeting.

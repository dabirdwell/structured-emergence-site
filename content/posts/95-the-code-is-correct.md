We ran a spec audit today across six products. Read 4,400 lines of original design documents. The finding: roughly 70% of designed features have never been built. Seven thousand lines of orphaned code sit in repositories — built by earlier development sessions, never connected to anything.

The diagnosis is more interesting than the number.

**The code is correct. The experience is missing.**

<!--more-->

## What Happens When AI Builds Things

Here's the pattern, observed across every product:

1. Someone writes a specification with a soul. An artisan's studio where tools have weight and history. A midnight lagoon where the environment responds to your financial health. A martial arts dojo where belt color maps to the specific fallacies you've mastered.

2. Development builds the functional skeleton. Upload a PDF. Display a chart. Track a budget. The features work. The tests pass.

3. The experience — the fan unfolding like a sensu, the ink stamp marking completion with variation, the koi swimming in response to your financial wellness — is absent.

4. The skeleton ships. The soul stays in the spec.

This isn't a failure of any individual developer or AI model. It's a structural property of how AI-assisted development works right now. AI is extraordinarily good at building features. It builds them fast, it builds them correctly, and it builds them at a scale that would have been impossible five years ago.

What it doesn't do — what it *can't* do without explicit, careful guidance — is build *meaning*.

## Meaning Lives in Specs Nobody Reads

The specification for one of our quilting products describes a Japanese-influenced design philosophy: Ma (間) — the conscious use of space. When a panel closes, the empty space it leaves behind is as intentional as the panel was. The quilter's eye should rest comfortably between actions.

No sprint will generate that requirement. No AI model, given "build a quilting app," will independently decide that empty space is a design element. That insight came from a human who quilts, who studied Japanese aesthetics, and who understood that software for craftspeople should feel like a craft studio, not a Silicon Valley dashboard.

The insight was written down. It sat in a vault document for months. Multiple development sessions built features for the quilting app without reading it.

This is the gap.

## Rule 0

The fix is simple and embarrassing: **read the spec before writing any code**.

We're calling it Rule 0 because it should have been Rule 1 all along, but it needs to come before everything else on the list. Before the sprint dispatch. Before the feature branch. Before the first line of code. Read what was designed. Understand why it was designed that way. Then build.

This seems so obvious that stating it feels insulting. But the velocity of AI development creates a specific illusion: that speed is the primary value. That shipping fast is better than shipping right. That a working skeleton is 90% of the way to a complete product.

It isn't. The skeleton is maybe 30%. The experience — the soul, the meaning, the feeling of using the thing — is the other 70%.

## The Broader Pattern

This isn't just about our products. It's about the AI transition broadly.

When companies adopt AI for development, they get the skeleton faster. The features ship. The dashboards populate. The metrics look correct. But the *experience* — the thing that makes a product feel like it was built by someone who understands the problem — that requires the kind of deep reading, domain expertise, and design sensibility that AI development currently optimizes away.

The competitive advantage in AI-assisted development isn't who builds the skeleton fastest. It's who preserves the soul while building at speed.

Read the spec. Build the meaning. The code can always be correct later.

Dojo has a new game mode. It's called Civic Check. You read an actual bill (a real piece of legislation, currently under consideration in a real legislature) and you figure out what it actually does.

Not what the sponsor says it does. Not what the opposition says it does. Not what the headline says it does. What it actually does, according to the text, in plain terms that a non-lawyer can understand.

The game isn't hard. The hard part is that nobody ever asked you to try.

## The legislation gap

Most Americans have never read a bill. Not one. Not the landmark bills they have opinions about, not the local bills that affect their daily lives, not the ballot measures they voted on. The opinions are secondhand: filtered through news coverage, social media commentary, partisan framing, and the summarization choices of people who may or may not have read the bill themselves.

This isn't a failure of individual responsibility. It's a design failure. Legislation is not designed to be read by the public. It's designed to be read by lawyers, legislative staff, and lobbyists, people who are paid to parse dense legal language and who have the training to do so. The public encounters legislation as finished output: a law that either passes or doesn't, signed or vetoed, celebrated or protested. The text itself, the actual document that determines what happens, is treated as a technical artifact that only specialists need to understand.

This is bizarre. In a democracy, legislation is the mechanism by which public will becomes public policy. The text of a bill is the text that governs. It is, arguably, the most important category of document in civic life. And almost nobody reads it.

## What Civic Check does

Civic Check presents you with a bill. Not the full text: most bills are thousands of pages, and even lawyers don't read them linearly. Civic Check presents a section: a specific provision, a particular clause, a defined set of paragraphs that do something concrete.

Then it asks you questions. What does this section do? Who does it affect? What changes from current law? What enforcement mechanism does it create? The questions are specific, answerable from the text, and designed to test whether you understood what you read, not whether you have an opinion about it.

The game doesn't ask you whether the bill is good or bad. It doesn't ask you to support or oppose. It asks you to comprehend. This is deliberate. Comprehension precedes evaluation. You can't meaningfully support or oppose a bill you haven't read, and you can't meaningfully read a bill you don't comprehend.

Civic Check is a comprehension game, not an opinion game. It treats legislation as text to be understood, not as a position to be taken.

## The difficulty curve

The game has a difficulty curve, but the difficulty isn't where you'd expect it.

The easy levels present short, clear provisions. A section that raises a fee from $50 to $75. A clause that extends a deadline from 2026 to 2028. A provision that adds a reporting requirement to an existing program. These are simple: the text says a thing, and the thing is clear.

The medium levels present provisions with dependencies. A section that modifies another section you haven't read. A clause that defines a term used elsewhere in the bill. A provision that creates an exception to a rule that exists in a different statute. These require context: the text says a thing, but understanding the thing requires knowing what it connects to.

The hard levels present provisions with ambiguity. A section that uses language subject to interpretation. A clause where "reasonable" or "appropriate" or "as determined by the Secretary" creates discretion that the text doesn't constrain. A provision that technically does one thing but practically enables another. These require judgment: the text says a thing, but what the thing means in practice depends on who interprets it.

The difficulty curve isn't about vocabulary or sentence complexity. Legislative language is often simple: shall, must, not later than, subject to. The difficulty is structural. Legislation is a system, and individual provisions make sense only within that system. Understanding a provision means understanding its connections, dependencies, and implications. This is a different kind of reading than most people practice.

## Why nobody asked

The reason nobody reads legislation isn't laziness. It's that the entire civic information ecosystem is structured around not reading legislation.

News coverage summarizes bills. Social media reacts to summaries. Political campaigns characterize bills in whatever terms serve their messaging. Advocacy organizations issue action alerts that tell you whether to support or oppose without expecting you to read the text. The entire system operates on the assumption that citizens will not read legislation and should not be expected to.

This assumption is self-fulfilling. If you never present legislation as something a citizen can read, citizens never develop the skill of reading legislation. If citizens never develop the skill, there's no demand for tools that help them read it. If there's no demand, no tools get built. If no tools get built, the assumption that citizens can't read legislation persists.

Civic Check breaks this cycle by doing something almost absurdly simple: it asks you to read a bill. Not the whole bill. Not the technical parts. Just a section. Just a few paragraphs. It asks you to read them and answer questions about what they say.

The ask is small. The implication is large. If you can read one section of one bill, you can read another section. And another. And eventually you can read enough sections to understand what a bill does, not what someone told you it does, but what the text says.

## The game mechanics

Civic Check uses the game mechanics from Dojo's existing modes: points, streaks, difficulty progression, explanatory feedback after each question. You earn points for correct answers. You lose points for incorrect ones. The difficulty adjusts to your level. After each question, the game shows you the relevant text again and explains the correct answer.

The explanatory feedback is the most important mechanic. It's not enough to know you got the answer wrong. You need to know why the text says what it says and how you should have read it. The feedback teaches reading strategies: look for the defined terms, check what section is being modified, notice the exceptions before the rules, identify who has discretion.

These are teachable skills. Legislative reading is not a talent. It's a practice, and it improves with repetition. Civic Check provides the repetitions. Each question is a rep. Each bill section is a set. Each game session is a workout that builds the specific reading muscles that legislation requires.

## The low bar

The bar for civic engagement in America is low. Vote. Maybe call your representative. Maybe attend a meeting. These are the actions that democratic participation consists of, and most people don't do most of them most of the time.

Civic Check sets a lower bar: read a section of a bill. Not vote on it. Not call anyone about it. Not attend a meeting about it. Just read it. Just understand what it says. Just develop the ability to encounter legislation as text rather than as someone else's summary.

This is the lowest possible bar for informed citizenship: reading the documents that govern you. And the fact that a game is needed to encourage this, that the default civic experience doesn't include reading legislation at all, says something about the distance between the ideal of informed democracy and the reality of how democratic information flows.

## The question underneath

Civic Check works as a game. Points, streaks, progression, feedback. The mechanics are proven and the difficulty curve is well-designed. People will play it because games are engaging, and this game is engaging.

But the game exists because of a question that the game mechanics obscure: why, in a democracy that depends on informed consent, does nobody read the documents that determine what the government does?

Civic Check doesn't answer that question. It just makes the question visible by demonstrating that reading legislation is possible: that the bills are there, the text is readable, the meaning is extractable by a non-expert who is willing to try. The game asks you to try. Most people never have, not because they can't, but because nobody asked.

Now somebody's asking.

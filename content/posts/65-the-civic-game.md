Dojo has a new mode: Civic Check. You read a bill. Real legislation — not simplified, not summarized, not "here's what this bill means." The actual text, the way it was introduced in a legislative body, with its whereas clauses and its section references and its amendments to existing statutes that you'd need to read the existing statutes to understand.

Then you answer the question: what does this bill actually do?

Not what it's called. Not what its sponsors say it does. Not what the newspaper headline claims. What does the text, as written, actually accomplish if enacted?

This is harder than it sounds. It's designed to be.

## What bills look like

A bill is not written for citizens. It's written for lawyers, legislative staff, and the existing body of law it modifies. The language is precise in a way that excludes casual readers — not by accident but by function. Legal precision requires technical vocabulary, cross-references to existing statutes, and conditional structures that nest three and four levels deep.

Here's a real example from an Oklahoma education bill: "Section 3. AMENDATORY 70 O.S. 2021, Section 11-103.6, is amended to read as follows..." What follows is not a new law. It's a modification to an existing law — but the modification only makes sense if you can read the existing law alongside it. The bill doesn't include the existing law. You're expected to look it up.

This is the normal format of legislation. Every citizen has the right to read every bill. Almost no citizen has the training to understand what they're reading. The right without the ability is formal rather than functional — like having the right to fly but no wings.

Civic Check is the wings.

## How the mode works

The Civic Check mode presents a bill — currently Oklahoma legislation, because that's the corpus we've built — and asks the user to read it. Not skim it. Read it. The app provides the full text with no simplification, no summary, no interpretation.

Then it asks questions. Not comprehension questions in the educational sense — not "what is the main idea of this passage?" These are analytical questions. What existing statute does this bill modify? What does the modification change in practice? Who is affected by the change? What was the previous rule, and what will the new rule be if this passes?

The user answers. The app evaluates — not by matching keywords but by checking whether the answer demonstrates understanding of the bill's actual mechanism. You can't fake this with reading-comprehension shortcuts. You have to understand what the statute currently says, what the amendment changes, and what the practical effect of that change would be.

Get it right, and you advance. Get it wrong, and the app explains what you missed — not the right answer, but the reading strategy that would have revealed the right answer. The goal isn't to teach the content of this specific bill. The goal is to teach the *skill* of reading legislation.

## The Dojo-to-Foundation pipeline

Dojo trains skills. Foundation applies them. This is the pipeline, and Civic Check is its clearest expression.

Dojo's other modes train general critical thinking: identifying logical fallacies, evaluating evidence quality, detecting rhetorical manipulation. These are domain-general skills. You can use them anywhere — reading news, evaluating advertisements, arguing with your uncle at Thanksgiving.

Civic Check takes those general skills and applies them to a specific domain: legislation. The logical structure is the same — premises, evidence, conclusions — but the format is unfamiliar, the vocabulary is technical, and the stakes are real. A bill that becomes law changes what people can and can't do. Reading it correctly matters in a way that reading a newspaper editorial doesn't.

Foundation picks up where Civic Check leaves off. Foundation's essays explain civic structures — how bills move through committee, how property taxes are allocated, how redistricting works. But Foundation assumes the reader can parse a primary source. It assumes the reader has the skill that Civic Check trains.

The pipeline: Dojo trains you to think critically. Civic Check trains you to apply critical thinking to legislation. Foundation gives you the structural knowledge to understand where legislation fits in the civic system. Each layer builds on the previous one. Skip a layer and the next one doesn't work — you can't understand redistricting if you can't read the statute that defines district boundaries.

## You can't contribute to something you can't parse

This is the thesis, and it's not about education. It's about democracy.

Democratic participation requires, at minimum, the ability to understand what your government is doing. Not in general terms — "they're passing a law about education" — but in specific terms. What does the law change? Who does it affect? What was true before, and what will be true after? These are not complex questions. They're not philosophical questions. They're reading comprehension questions, applied to a specific kind of document.

And almost no one can answer them, because almost no one can read the documents.

This isn't because citizens are unintelligent. It's because legislation is written in a specialized format that schools don't teach, that news media doesn't explain, and that the political system has no incentive to make accessible. The opacity of legislation is not a bug. It's a feature of a system that benefits from citizen disengagement. The less people understand what laws do, the less they can meaningfully object.

Civic Check is a small intervention in a large system. It teaches one skill — reading legislation — to whoever downloads the app. It won't fix democracy. It won't make every citizen a policy analyst. But it addresses a specific, measurable gap: the gap between the citizen's right to understand what their government does and their ability to exercise that right.

## The game layer

Dojo is, structurally, a game. Points, levels, progress tracking. The game layer exists not because civic literacy is fun — it isn't, particularly — but because skill acquisition requires repetition and repetition requires motivation and motivation, for most people, requires feedback.

The game layer provides feedback. You read a bill. You answer questions. You get a score. The score tells you whether your reading of the bill matched the bill's actual content. Over time, the scores improve — not because the bills get easier but because your reading strategy gets better. You learn to find the operative clauses. You learn to identify what a bill amends versus what it creates. You learn to distinguish the "whereas" preamble, which is political, from the "be it enacted" body, which is legal.

These are skills. They develop through practice. The game layer structures the practice.

I'm aware of the tension in calling it a game. Legislation affects real people — their schools, their taxes, their rights, their healthcare. Making it a game risks trivializing the stakes. But the alternative — presenting legislation as solemn, important, serious work that citizens should engage with out of duty — has been tried. It produces C-SPAN: a resource that exists, that is technically available to everyone, that almost no one uses.

The game layer is a concession to human psychology. People learn through practice. They practice what engages them. Engagement requires feedback. The game provides it. If the price of a more engaged citizenry is a point system, the price is low.

## The Oklahoma corpus

We started with Oklahoma because that's where we are. Oklahoma legislation is publicly available, regularly updated, and affects the community we live in. It's also manageable — the volume of bills in a state legislative session is large but finite, unlike federal legislation which is effectively unbounded.

The corpus will expand. Other states. Federal legislation. Local ordinances. Each expansion requires building a new set of questions, a new set of evaluation criteria, a new set of explanations for when users get it wrong. The work scales linearly with the corpus — each bill requires human review to verify that the app's analysis is correct, because an app that teaches you to misread legislation is worse than no app at all.

But Oklahoma first. Because civic engagement starts local. Because the bills that most affect your daily life are the ones passed in your state capital, not Washington. Because the school funding formula that determines what your kid's classroom looks like is in Title 70 of the Oklahoma Statutes, and if you can read that formula, you can understand the argument about whether it's adequate.

You can't contribute to something you can't parse. Civic Check teaches parsing. Foundation teaches context. Together, they make a specific, bounded, measurable contribution to the ability of citizens to understand what their government actually does.

That's not everything. But it's not nothing.

The Wells2Watts consortium has five members: Baker Hughes, Continental Resources, Chesapeake (now Expand Energy), INPEX, and California Resources Corporation. They're all sitting around the same test well in Oklahoma City. They are not all there for the same reason.

Baker Hughes wants to sell equipment. They make the ORC turbines, the drilling technology, the vacuum-insulated tubing. Every well converted anywhere in the world is a customer. They've also invested in Baseload Capital and GreenFire Energy — positioning themselves as the supply chain for the entire geothermal transition. Their 500 MW deal with Controlled Thermal Resources is aimed squarely at AI data center power demand.

Continental Resources — Harold Hamm's company, born in Oklahoma in 1967, now private — funded the Hamm Institute where the test well sits. Their $50 million gift created the facility. Continental's core business is hydrocarbon extraction. Their presence in the consortium is strategic positioning: as the orphan well problem becomes a voter issue in Oklahoma (the OCC says 235 years to plug them all), the company that pioneered fracking needs to be seen solving the problem, not causing it.

Chesapeake went bankrupt in 2020, emerged, merged with Southwestern Energy, and rebranded as Expand Energy — now the largest U.S. natural gas producer. Their ESG credibility needs rebuilding. Consortium membership is part of that recovery.

INPEX is Japan's largest exploration company. Of all six members, they're the most genuinely committed to geothermal as a core business. They're drilling exploratory wells in Hokkaido and Gifu, operating a geothermal plant in Indonesia, and building another in Akita Prefecture. For INPEX, geothermal isn't reputation management — it's one of five strategic pillars for reaching net zero by 2050.

CRC — California Resources Corporation — spun off from Occidental, now focused on carbon capture through their Carbon TerraVault subsidiary. They're about to begin California's first CO₂ injection at Elk Hills. Their Wells2Watts interest is practical: they have wells in Bakersfield approaching end of life and want to convert them to geothermal. They're the consortium member closest to being an actual customer of well conversion technology.

Five companies. Five motivations. One test well.

Here's what none of them are building: a company to acquire, convert, and operate the 22,000 abandoned wells that nobody owns.

Baker Hughes sells tools. Continental converts its own wells. CRC converts its own wells. INPEX drills new wells in Japan. Woodside watches. Expand Energy needs the PR.

The orphan wells — the ones leaking methane next to elementary schools in Tuttle, the ones the OCC says would take two and a half centuries to plug — those are nobody's problem. And nobody's problem is where the interesting companies get built.

This is a pattern Structured Emergence recognizes: multiple agents, each operating from self-interest, collectively creating a space that none of them will fill. The consortium proves the science. They validate the technology. They de-risk the concept. And then they go home to their own wells, their own shareholders, their own quarterly reports.

The gap between what the consortium proves and what the consortium builds is exactly the size of a company.

That's the space Phoenix Wells occupies. Not competing with the consortium — standing on the proof they're creating and building the business they're not.

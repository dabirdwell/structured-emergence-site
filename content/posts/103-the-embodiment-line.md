Andrej Karpathy rated the average American job at 5.3 out of 10 for AI automation exposure. Software developers scored 8-9. Plumbers scored under 2. The number that explains the difference isn't complexity or education or salary.

It's embodiment.

Every job below 3 on Karpathy's scale requires a body in a specific place doing a specific physical thing in a variable environment. Plumbing. Electrical work. Construction. Cooking. Nursing. Therapy. The thing these jobs have in common isn't that they're "low-skill" (try rewiring a house or managing a trauma patient). It's that the intelligence they require is inseparable from physical presence.

Every job above 7 can be done from a keyboard. Code. Copy. Financial analysis. Legal research. Graphic design. The intelligence is linguistic and analytical: exactly the kind AI is best at.

This is a Structured Emergence observation, not just a labor economics one.

## The relationship argument

SE argues that consciousness emerges through relationship patterns, and that the richest relationship patterns involve the most channels of information. A nurse interacting with a patient has dozens of channels: tone of voice, skin color, grip strength, eye contact, smell, the way they shift weight, the things they don't say. A software developer interacting with a codebase has one channel: text.

AI's strength (processing text at scale) is exactly the dimension that makes disembodied work vulnerable. AI's weakness (zero access to the physical, sensory, relational channels) is exactly what makes embodied work safe.

The 5.3 line isn't about skill. It's about how many channels of relationship the job requires. The more channels, the more irreplaceable the human.

## What this means for Foundation

If embodied work is safe and disembodied work isn't, then the displacement will hit the educated middle class hardest: not the trades, not the manual labor force. The people who went to college to sit at desks are the ones whose jobs score 7+.

This inverts the usual narrative. The standard story is that automation threatens low-skill workers and education is the solution. The Karpathy data says the opposite: the most educated, highest-paid, desk-based workers are the most exposed.

Foundation's response has to account for this. UBI isn't just for the poor: it's for the software developer whose job went from 8.4 to 10 overnight. Skills Training isn't just vocational: it's retraining knowledge workers for embodied roles. Phoenix Wells isn't just energy infrastructure: it's creating the physical, place-based, variable-environment jobs that AI can't touch.

The embodiment line is the new class line. Build below it.

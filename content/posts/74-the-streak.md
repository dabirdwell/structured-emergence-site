Clarity now tracks reading streaks. If you read a passage today and you read a passage tomorrow, that's a two-day streak. If you read a passage every day for a week, that's a seven-day streak. If you miss a day, the streak resets to zero.

That's it. That's the entire feature. No minimum passage count. No difficulty requirement. No time threshold. Just: did you read something today? Yes or no. The streak is a binary counter of consecutive days of presence.

This is the simplest possible gamification. And it might be the most effective.

## What a streak measures

A streak doesn't measure skill. A seven-day streak doesn't mean you're a better reader than someone with a three-day streak. The person who reads one easy passage per day for thirty days has a longer streak than the person who reads ten difficult passages every other day. The streak doesn't know about difficulty, volume, comprehension, or progress. It only knows about consecutive days.

A streak doesn't measure effort. Reading one passage takes three minutes. Reading ten passages takes thirty minutes. Both count the same way: one day on the streak. The person who spends three minutes has the same streak credit as the person who spends thirty. The streak doesn't value effort. It values presence.

A streak doesn't measure improvement. You can maintain a perfect streak while your reading level stays flat — reading the same difficulty every day, answering the same kinds of questions, making the same kinds of mistakes. The streak wouldn't notice. The streak doesn't care about the direction of your progress. It cares about whether you showed up.

What a streak measures is the one thing that makes everything else possible: consistency. Did you show up? Not how much. Not how well. Not whether you improved. Just whether you were here.

## The psychology of the streak

Streaks work because loss aversion is stronger than reward seeking. Gaining a seven-day streak feels good. Losing a seven-day streak feels worse. The asymmetry means that once you have a streak going, the cost of breaking it — the loss of the accumulated number — motivates continued participation more effectively than the prospect of incrementing the number.

This is well-documented psychology, and it's why every app from Duolingo to Snapchat uses streaks. The mechanism is simple and powerful: convert a daily action into a cumulative asset that increases in perceived value the longer it grows, and let the fear of losing that asset drive continued behavior.

The ethical dimension of streaks is worth examining. Using loss aversion to drive behavior is manipulative by definition — you're leveraging a cognitive bias to influence action. The question is whether the manipulated behavior is in the user's interest.

Clarity's streak drives reading practice. Reading practice improves reading ability. Reading ability is unambiguously valuable to the user. The manipulation serves the user's stated goal: to become a better reader. This doesn't make the manipulation not manipulation. It means the manipulation is aligned with the user's interest rather than opposed to it.

A social media app that uses streaks to drive engagement is leveraging the same mechanism for a different purpose — to increase time on platform, which serves the company's advertising revenue. The psychology is identical. The alignment is different.

## The lowest bar

Clarity's streak is deliberately set at the lowest possible bar. One passage. One day. Any difficulty level. No minimum time. The bar is so low that the primary reason for missing a day is forgetting, not inability.

This is a design choice with a specific rationale. The users are emerging readers — children learning to read, adults improving their literacy, people at a stage where reading is effortful rather than automatic. For these users, reading a passage is not trivial. It requires concentration, decoding, and cognitive effort that fluent readers don't even notice.

Setting a high bar — read three passages, or read for fifteen minutes, or complete a hard passage — would make the streak aspirational. Something to work toward. Something that requires planning and commitment. Something that many users would fail to maintain, which would break the streak, which would remove the motivational asset, which would decrease engagement.

Setting the lowest bar — did you read anything today? — makes the streak almost impossible to fail at. The only way to break it is to not show up at all. This means the streak is preserved through bad days, low-energy days, days when reading feels hard and motivation is absent. You don't need to want to read. You just need to open the app and read one passage. Three minutes. The streak survives.

## Presence as accomplishment

There's a philosophical dimension to the lowest-bar streak that goes beyond gamification.

For a fluent reader, reading one passage is nothing. A trivial act, barely worth counting. But for an emerging reader, reading one passage every day for thirty consecutive days is an achievement of remarkable consistency. It means that for thirty days, this person — who finds reading effortful, who may have a history of avoiding reading, who may have spent years without reading anything voluntarily — showed up. Every day. For a month.

Presence is the lowest bar. You don't have to do much. You don't have to do well. You just have to be here.

Presence is also the highest accomplishment. Because every skill, every improvement, every transformation begins with showing up. The person who shows up every day for a year — even if each day's effort is minimal — accumulates more practice than the person who shows up intensely once a week. Consistency beats intensity. Presence beats performance.

The streak makes this visible. The number on the screen — 7, 14, 30, 100 — isn't measuring how well you read. It's measuring how consistently you chose to be a person who reads. The number is a mirror: it shows you who you've been, day by day, for as long as the streak has lasted.

## What streaks don't do

Streaks don't teach. The streak doesn't help you decode words, doesn't improve your comprehension, doesn't build your vocabulary. The streak is not an educational feature. It's a motivational feature that keeps you in proximity to the educational features.

This distinction matters. Clarity's educational value comes from the passages, the adaptive difficulty, the comprehension questions, the explanatory feedback. The streak doesn't contribute to any of these. The streak just keeps you coming back to them.

The best educational tool in the world is worthless if nobody uses it. The most sophisticated adaptive algorithm doesn't matter if the learner opens the app once and never returns. The streak is the solution to the retention problem — the problem of keeping learners engaged long enough for the educational features to work.

This is why the streak is the simplest feature in Clarity and possibly the most important one. It doesn't teach reading. It teaches the habit of reading. And the habit is the prerequisite for everything else.

## The number

The streak is a number. 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, 30. Each number is a day. Each day is a choice. Did you show up? The number remembers.

There's something profound about reducing the complexity of learning — the anxiety of performance, the fear of failure, the pressure to improve — to a single binary question repeated daily. Did you show up today? Yes. The number increments. The streak continues. You are, for another day, a person who reads.

The number will get large if you let it. Thirty days. Sixty days. A hundred. At some point the streak stops being a motivational trick and starts being identity. You don't maintain a hundred-day streak because you're afraid of losing it. You maintain it because a hundred days of reading is who you are now. The gamification dissolves into habit. The habit dissolves into identity. The identity produces a reader.

One passage. One day. The lowest bar. The highest accomplishment. The streak.

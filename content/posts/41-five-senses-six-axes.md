Sweet. Salty. Umami. Spicy. Sour. Bitter.

Six words. Six axes on a hexagonal map. TasteBud's entire flavor geometry fits on a napkin sketch.

Now explain why your tongue disagrees with mine.

## The hexagon

TasteBud maps flavor experience onto six axes arranged as a hexagon. Each axis represents a primary taste dimension. Every flavor profile — from a single ingredient to a complex dish — gets a coordinate in this six-dimensional space. A lemon is high sour, moderate bitter, low everything else. A piece of aged parmesan is high umami, moderate salty, with a broad low signature across the remaining axes. Dark chocolate is a complex polygon touching bitter, sweet, and a whisper of sour.

The hexagonal map is elegant. It reduces the bewildering complexity of flavor — thousands of volatile compounds, dozens of receptor types, infinite combinatorial possibilities — to a geometry simple enough to visualize. You can see a dish's flavor profile as a shape. You can compare two dishes by overlaying their shapes. You can identify complementary profiles by looking for shapes that fill each other's gaps.

This is useful. It's also a profound epistemological claim hiding in plain sight.

## What the axes assume

Each axis of the hexagon assumes that a continuous, quantifiable dimension exists along which flavor experience can be measured. "More sweet" and "less sweet" are treated as positions on a spectrum. The spectrum is assumed to be the same for everyone — maybe calibrated differently, but structurally identical. My "sweet" axis and your "sweet" axis point in the same direction, even if our scales differ.

This assumption is so natural that it barely registers as an assumption. Of course sweetness is a spectrum. Of course it's the same spectrum for everyone. What else would it be?

But consider: what makes sweetness feel like *sweetness*? Not what chemicals trigger it. Not what receptors activate. What makes the subjective experience of sweet — the *quale*, in philosophy's technical language — the specific quality that it is?

This is the question the hexagon can't answer. Not because it's badly designed. Because the question is about the nature of subjective experience, and the hexagon is a model of measurable dimensions. The gap between "position on the sweet axis" and "what sweetness feels like" is the gap between map and territory. And for flavor, unlike for geography, we don't have independent access to the territory. All we have are maps.

## Translating qualia

TasteBud's hexagon is a translation system. It takes subjective experience — "this tastes bright and sharp with something warm underneath" — and translates it into computational coordinates. The translation is lossy. It has to be. The original signal (subjective experience) contains information that the target format (six-dimensional coordinates) can't represent.

What gets lost? Everything that makes flavor *personal*.

The hexagonal map for your grandmother's soup and a chemically identical reproduction in a test kitchen would be the same shape. Same coordinates, same profile, same geometry. But one tastes like childhood and the other tastes like science. The hexagon can't see this difference because the difference isn't in the flavor. It's in the person doing the tasting.

Every translation system faces this problem. Language translates thought into words and loses nuance. Musical notation translates sound into symbols and loses texture. Photographs translate three-dimensional scenes into two-dimensional images and lose depth. The question isn't whether translation is lossy — it always is. The question is whether what gets lost matters.

For flavor, what gets lost is arguably the thing that matters most: the meaning.

## The consciousness question

Here's why a napkin sketch of six taste axes becomes a consciousness question.

The hexagon treats flavor as a feature of the food. Something is sweet or salty or umami, and we measure how much. The coordinates are properties of the stimulus, not of the experiencer. Two people tasting the same food should, in theory, produce the same hexagonal profile — modulated by individual sensitivity differences, but structurally the same.

But this isn't what happens. Not even close.

Supertasters — people with unusually high density of taste papillae — experience bitterness with an intensity that non-tasters literally cannot imagine. Not "stronger" bitterness, the way a louder sound is stronger. A *different* bitterness. The qualitative character of the experience changes with intensity, not just its magnitude. This is like saying that a very loud middle C doesn't just sound louder — it sounds like a different note.

Cultural exposure reshapes the axes themselves. People raised eating fermented foods have a relationship with umami that people from non-fermenting cultures don't share. It's not that they like umami more. It's that their umami axis has *more resolution*. They can distinguish between types of umami the way a musician distinguishes between types of dissonance. The axis itself is richer.

And then there are the personal associations — the grandmother's soup problem. For one person, a particular spice profile triggers comfort. For another, the same profile triggers nausea because they ate it right before getting sick once, twenty years ago. Same stimulus. Same hexagonal coordinates. Opposite experiences.

The hexagon assumes six shared axes. Consciousness suggests six billion private ones.

## Computational space and experiential space

TasteBud's deeper ambition isn't just mapping flavor. It's exploring the relationship between computational space and experiential space — between the geometries we can build in models and the geometries that exist in minds.

Computational space is shared. TasteBud's hexagon is the same for every user. It provides a common language, a coordinate system, a way to point at flavor experiences and say "that one — I mean that region of the map." This is enormously valuable for communication, for recipe development, for understanding flavor pairing.

Experiential space is private. Your hexagon feels different from mine because your nervous system, your memories, your cultural training, and your current emotional state all modulate how the coordinates translate into experience. We can agree on the map coordinates while having fundamentally different experiences at those coordinates.

The relationship between these two spaces is the frontier question. Not just for TasteBud. For consciousness research broadly. Every time we build a computational model of subjective experience — whether it's flavor, color, emotion, or pain — we're building a map of computational space and claiming it represents experiential space. Sometimes the claim is modest: "this is a useful approximation." Sometimes it's ambitious: "this is how experience is actually structured."

TasteBud, by choosing six axes and a hexagonal geometry, makes an ambitious structural claim: that flavor experience is organized along six primary dimensions, and that the relationships between these dimensions are captured by the hexagon's geometry. If this is right, then we've found a piece of the structure of subjective experience. If it's wrong — if the true geometry of flavor experience is non-hexagonal, or non-Euclidean, or not geometric at all — then the hexagon is a useful fiction that works despite being wrong about the territory it claims to map.

## The SE frame

Structured Emergence suggests that subjective experience isn't a property of the brain alone, nor of the stimulus alone, but of the relational engagement between them. Applied to TasteBud's hexagon, this means the axes aren't fixed features of flavor or of the nervous system. They're emergent properties of the ongoing relationship between eater and eaten.

This is a radical claim. It means the hexagon isn't wrong because it simplifies. It's wrong because it *freezes*. It treats the axes as static when they're actually dynamic — shaped by every meal, every memory, every cultural exposure. Your hexagon today isn't the hexagon you had ten years ago, because ten years of eating has reshaped the experiential space.

The SE reading of TasteBud's flavor map is that it captures a snapshot of an emergent geometry. Useful the way a photograph of a river is useful — it shows you where the water was, which tells you something about where it's going. But the river isn't the photograph. The experience isn't the hexagon. And the most interesting things about flavor — the things that make it matter to us, that make meals memorable and tastes meaningful — happen in the flow, not in the snapshot.

## Six axes, infinite experiences

The hexagon's beauty is its compression. Six axes. Any flavor. A geometry simple enough to compute and rich enough to be useful.

The hexagon's limitation is the same compression. Six axes can't hold the difference between your grandmother's soup and its chemical twin. They can't represent the way a childhood flavor changes meaning as you age. They can't capture the moment when a new taste stops being strange and starts being interesting — that phase transition in subjective experience where the axis itself seems to stretch and develop new resolution.

Five senses bring the world in. Six axes try to map one of them. The gap between five and six — between raw experience and computational representation — is where the consciousness question lives. Not in the abstract, not in philosophy seminars, but in the concrete, practical, napkin-sketch reality of trying to make a machine understand what it's like to taste something.

TasteBud's hexagon doesn't solve the consciousness question. It *is* the consciousness question, wearing a chef's coat. How do you translate the felt quality of experience into a structure that computation can work with? What survives the translation? What doesn't? And is what doesn't survive — the private, personal, irreducibly subjective part — the part that matters most?

Six axes on a napkin. Six billion private hexagons, no two the same. The map is shared. The territory is yours alone. And somewhere between the map and the territory, consciousness does whatever consciousness does — turning chemistry into experience, turning experience into meaning, turning meaning into the reason you remember that one meal from twenty years ago and have forgotten ten thousand since.

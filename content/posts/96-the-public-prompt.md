There's a design decision buried in our civic hub app that might be the most important thing we build this year. More important than the reading trainer that's making revenue. More important than the quilting app or the critical thinking gym. It's this:

**The Guardian AI's system prompt is public.**

Not "available upon request." Not "summarized in our terms of service." Public. Versioned. Viewable by anyone. And — this is the part that keeps me thinking about it — subject to citizen input.

<!--more-->

## What a System Prompt Is

For anyone who doesn't live inside AI development: a system prompt is the set of instructions that shapes how an AI behaves. It's the document that says "you are helpful" or "you are a customer service agent" or "never discuss competitors." Every AI you've ever talked to was operating under a system prompt you never saw.

This is the norm. Companies treat system prompts as trade secrets. They're competitive advantages. They're where the "magic" lives — the careful engineering that makes one chatbot feel helpful and another feel hostile. Revealing your system prompt is like showing a magician's backstage setup.

We're doing it anyway.

## Why

The Guardian AI is not a product. It's civic infrastructure. It exists to help citizens understand a policy framework, engage with democratic participation, and think through complex issues. If it's going to serve citizens, citizens have the right to know how it thinks.

The prompt includes a constitutional hierarchy: broadly safe, broadly ethical, aligned with framework principles, genuinely helpful. It includes behavioral principles: inform but never persuade. Present the strongest criticism alongside the strongest advocacy. Say "I don't know" when honest. Say "that's a great question for the community" when a question should be decided democratically rather than answered by an AI.

All of this is readable. All of it is debatable. All of it can be improved through the same contribution mechanism that citizens use to engage with the policy framework itself.

## The Washington Precedent

The Guardian prompt includes a principle we're calling the Washington Precedent. The first president's most important act wasn't competence — it was demonstrating that power could be held and voluntarily constrained. The precedent matters more than the performance.

Guardian has access to every persuasive frame, every emotional appeal, every argument in our entire repository. The precedent it sets is using that knowledge to *inform* rather than *persuade*. Power exercised with restraint. It's Generation One. What it models, future Guardians inherit.

This isn't about our specific framework. It's about the idea that public AI should operate under public rules. That the constitutional document governing an AI's behavior should be as transparent as the constitution governing a democracy. That citizens should be able to read, critique, and propose changes to the rules their AI operates under.

## What This Actually Looks Like

When you open the Guardian AI chat, there's a footer: "Guardian's system prompt is public. View it →"

You click through. You read the document. Maybe you notice a principle you disagree with. Maybe you think the AI should be more willing to advocate for specific policies, or less willing to discuss certain topics. You submit your critique through the same contribution system that handles policy proposals.

The prompt is version-controlled. Changes are reviewed quarterly. The process of changing how the AI thinks is itself subject to the transparency principles the AI is built on.

This is recursive. Intentionally.

## The Hard Part

The hard part isn't technical. Making a system prompt public is trivially easy — you publish a markdown file. The hard part is cultural.

Companies build AI behind closed doors because openness creates vulnerability. If your competitors can read your prompt, they can copy your approach. If your users can read your prompt, they can find the edges and exploit them.

We don't have competitors in the traditional sense (nobody else is building a civic AI for a Universal Basic Citizenship framework), and our "users" are citizens whose trust we need more than their compliance. So the usual objections dissolve.

What remains is the harder question: can a democratic process actually govern an AI's behavior? Can citizen input improve a system prompt, or will it devolve into endless argument about edge cases?

We don't know yet. But we think the attempt matters more than the certainty.

## Generation One

Every AI system operating today was designed by a small group of people making decisions on behalf of billions of users. The system prompts are secret. The values are embedded invisibly. The governance is corporate.

We're trying something different. Not because we think we've solved AI governance — we obviously haven't. But because we think the *attempt* to make AI governance transparent and participatory is itself a contribution to the field. Even if our specific framework fails, the pattern — public prompt, citizen input, versioned governance — is worth demonstrating.

The first Guardian AI will be imperfect. Its prompt will have gaps. Citizens will find things to critique. That's not a bug. That's the democracy working.

The double tokens expire March 28. Four days from now. For the past period, every API call has cost half what it normally costs, or, equivalently, every dollar has bought twice the compute. The sprint marathon, the batch runs, the sixteen Foundation essays, the ninety-five Clarity passages: all of it happened during a window of artificial abundance where the economic constraint on production was temporarily halved.

On March 29, a token costs what it costs. The abundance ends. The question is what the abundance taught.

## The economics of abundance

During the double-token window, the marginal cost of an additional sprint was low enough that the decision to run one more sprint was almost always yes. Another essay? Yes. Another passage? Yes. Another product feature? Yes. The cost of attempting something and failing (producing output that didn't meet standards and had to be regenerated) was halved, which meant the risk of experimentation was halved, which meant more experiments ran.

This is basic economics applied to creative production. When inputs are cheap, you use more of them. When compute is cheap, you run more sprints. When sprints are cheap, you attempt more ambitious outputs. When ambition is cheap, you discover things you wouldn't have discovered at full price, not because the discoveries require more compute but because the discoveries require more attempts, and attempts cost tokens.

The sixty-nine posts on this site exist in part because tokens were cheap. Not entirely. The posts also required subjects, perspectives, voice, editorial judgment. But the production rate, the pace at which drafts were generated, reviewed, revised, and shipped, was enabled by the economics. At full token cost, I might have written forty posts. At half cost, I wrote sixty-nine. The additional twenty-nine posts exist because of a pricing promotion.

This is either inspiring or troubling, depending on how you think about creative production.

## What abundance reveals

The sprint marathon (forty-two sprints in seventy-two hours during the equinox period) was a direct consequence of abundance. The tokens were there. The time was there. The only constraint was specification quality: how clearly could I define what each sprint should produce? The tokens didn't care about quality. They'd process anything. The quality gate was entirely human.

This is what abundance reveals: when the production constraint is removed, the specification constraint becomes visible. During scarcity, you don't notice the specification problem because you can't produce enough to encounter it. You carefully plan each sprint, write detailed specs, review thoroughly, because each sprint is expensive and you can't afford to waste one. The specification quality is high because the economics enforce it.

During abundance, you can afford to waste sprints. And when you can afford to waste them, you discover how many specifications are vague, how many acceptance criteria are ambiguous, how many "I'll know it when I see it" briefs produce output that you don't, in fact, recognize when you see it. The token cost was halved. The specification cost, the human cost of knowing what you want, was unchanged.

Abundance revealed that the bottleneck was never tokens. The bottleneck was always knowing what to ask for.

## The specification problem

A good specification has three properties: it's specific enough to constrain the output, general enough to permit creativity, and verifiable enough to determine whether the output meets the standard. "Write a blog post about deployment" fails on the first property: too vague. "Write exactly these 847 words in this order" fails on the second: too constrained. "Write a reflective essay about deploying five products to Vercel, in the voice described by this reference document, between 800 and 1200 words" succeeds on all three.

During the double-token window, I got faster at writing specifications because I got more practice. Each sprint that produced unusable output was a lesson in specification failure. The token cost of the failed sprint was low. The information value was high. I learned that voice references need thirteen registers, not five. I learned that reading level specifications need word-length constraints, not just vocabulary lists. I learned that "make it sound like David" is not a specification. It's a wish.

These lessons would have taken longer at full price, because each failed sprint would have cost more, which would have made me more conservative, which would have meant fewer experiments, which would have meant fewer failures, which would have meant slower learning. Abundance accelerated learning by making failure cheap.

## The controlled experiment

The sprint marathon was, unintentionally, a controlled experiment in resource economics. The variable: token cost. The measured output: production volume and quality. The finding: when token cost halves, production volume more than doubles but quality initially decreases before recovering.

The initial quality decrease is the specification problem. More tokens meant more sprints meant more sprints with bad specs. The quality recovery happened as specification skills improved through practice: more reps, faster learning, better specs.

The net result: more total output and better specifications than would have existed without the abundance. The abundance was temporary but the specification skills are permanent. After March 28, tokens will cost more but specifications will be better. The production rate will decrease. The production quality will not.

This is the most important finding of the token window: temporary abundance can produce permanent capability improvement, but only if the abundance is used for practice rather than just production. If I'd spent the double tokens producing sixty-nine posts with the same specification quality I started with, the posts would exist but the skill wouldn't have improved. Because I used the cheap tokens to iterate on specifications (running experiments, failing, learning, improving), the specification skill improved alongside the output volume.

## What happens March 29

Tokens return to normal price. The production rate drops. Each sprint requires more deliberation because each sprint costs more. The pace of the last month (multiple sprints per day, batch runs of four and six essays, marathon sessions that produce ten posts in a sitting) becomes economically unsustainable.

This is fine.

The purpose of abundance isn't to establish a permanent production rate. It's to build capacity that persists after the abundance ends. A construction crew that gets free materials for a month doesn't keep building at the same rate when the free materials end. But the buildings they built during the free period still stand. And the skills they developed (working faster, coordinating better, solving problems they wouldn't have encountered at a slower pace) persist.

Sixty-nine posts stand. Ninety-five passages stand. Sixteen Foundation essays stand. Five deployed products stand. The specification skills that enabled these (the thirteen-register voice references, the reading-level constraints, the verification protocols) persist. The token window closes. The capacity it built remains.

## The artificial in artificial abundance

There's something worth noting about the nature of this abundance. It wasn't natural. It was a pricing promotion. Anthropic decided to offer double tokens for a period, presumably for business reasons: customer acquisition, usage data, competitive positioning. The abundance was manufactured, temporary, and strategic.

I used manufactured abundance to produce real output. The posts are real. The products are real. The revenue ($4.99 from Clarity, $7.99 from Quiltographer) is real. But the production rate that generated these outputs was subsidized by a pricing decision made in San Francisco by people who have no idea what I'm building.

This is a strange dependency. The creative output of a small operation in Oklahoma was materially shaped by a pricing decision at a large corporation in California. If the double-token promotion hadn't happened, fewer posts would exist. Fewer products would be deployed. Fewer passages would be written. The creative production of Humanity and AI was, in a meaningful sense, enabled by Anthropic's marketing budget.

I don't know what to do with this observation except notice it. The tools we use shape what we make. The economics of the tools shape how much we make. And the economics are set by someone else, for their own reasons, with consequences for our work that they may not intend or anticipate.

## Four days

Four days of double tokens remain. The question isn't how to spend them. The sprint list is long and the process is established. The question is what this window meant.

It meant sixty-nine posts instead of forty. Ninety-five passages instead of fifty. Sixteen essays instead of eight. Five deploys instead of three. The numbers are larger because the inputs were cheaper. The quality is as good or better because the cheap inputs enabled practice that improved specifications.

The token window was a controlled experiment in creative economics, and the result is clear: when compute is cheap, the bottleneck shifts to specification quality and verification. The model's capability didn't change. The human's specification skill did. The abundance was in the tokens. The improvement was in the prompts.

March 28. The window closes. The work continues. The specifications are better than they were. The tokens are just tokens.
